Demand-adjusted probabilistic forecast evaluation

A statistical approach to interpreting probabilistic forecast errors across demand series with different scales.

RoleData Scientist
ContextKivos Analytics · Professional project
The challenge

Overview

Probabilistic forecasts represent a range of possible outcomes rather than a single predicted value. Their evaluation must therefore consider both the observed outcome and the uncertainty expressed by the forecast distribution.

The Continuous Ranked Probability Score (CRPS) is a proper scoring rule commonly used for this purpose. However, CRPS is expressed in the units of the predicted variable and is sensitive to its scale. Comparing raw CRPS values across demand series with substantially different levels of variability can therefore be misleading.

A higher CRPS does not necessarily indicate worse relative performance when the underlying forecasting tasks have different levels of uncertainty. Forecasts for more variable demand can have larger expected errors even when the predicted distributions describe that uncertainty appropriately.

The goal was to assess individual forecast errors in context, rather than interpreting their absolute CRPS values in isolation.

My work

My contribution

During an ongoing analysis, we identified the limitations of directly comparing CRPS across demand levels. I subsequently developed a statistical methodology for interpreting observed CRPS values relative to an empirical reference derived from historical forecast evaluations.

  • Investigated the relationship between demand magnitude and observed CRPS.
  • Developed a reference function describing the CRPS typically observed at different demand levels within comparable groups of forecasts.
  • Compared individual forecast scores with the corresponding reference to identify errors above or below historical expectations.
  • Designed the method as a complementary evaluation diagnostic without changing the underlying forecasting model.

Disclaimer

The adjusted comparison describes how a forecast performs relative to a historical reference; it is not an absolute measure of forecast quality. A forecast can perform as expected relative to a weak model while still being inaccurate or poorly calibrated. The interpretation depends on the representativeness and stability of the reference data and requires validation on unseen forecasts. Because the reference uses information about realized demand, the resulting diagnostic does not retain the proper-scoring guarantees of CRPS. It should therefore complement, rather than replace, raw CRPS, calibration checks, and other probabilistic forecast diagnostics.
